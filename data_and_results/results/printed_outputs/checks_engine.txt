== F2  The criteria are not one quantity: crossings carry almost no information about label occlusion
==============================================================================

All 30,060 layouts of the two engines on identical graphs, three corpora.
Spearman rho between the criteria, over figures (both engines pooled, so the
matrix describes the measurements, not one engine's habits).

The pair the paper's argument turns on: rho(arrow crossings, label collisions) = +0.11
  rho(bends per arrow, label collisions)   = -0.36
  rho(arrow length, label collisions)      = -0.07

Crossings, bends and length -- the criteria a layout tool optimises -- are
uncorrelated or negatively correlated with label occlusion, the criterion that
tracks the raters best (rho = 0.75, 5.3). Both grow with the graph, so within one
engine the raw correlation is positive; what matters is whether it survives
holding the graph fixed, below.

Within each engine separately, rho(crossings, label collisions):
  ours                   rho +0.16  (p=1.9e-34, n=6,012)
  dot (tb, curved)       rho +0.24  (p=2.4e-82, n=6,009)
  dot (tb, orthogonal)   rho +0.42  (p=4.6e-259, n=6,011)

With the number of boxes held fixed (rank-partial rho):
  rho(crossings, label collisions | boxes) = +0.02  (n=30,051)

  It does not. At a fixed graph size the two are independent, so a figure's
  crossing count says nothing about whether its labels can be read. This is the
  mechanism behind Table S2a: 'dot' wins the criterion this literature reports and
  loses the one that predicts a reader, and neither result implies the other.

== F3  Graphviz with an oracle over its four configurations
==============================================================================

on the identical graph. Table S2a reports the four configurations separately and
notes that no one of them gives both right angles and legible labels. The
stronger statement the same measurements support: give 'dot' the best of its four
configurations *per figure* -- an oracle it cannot have, since choosing needs the

== F4  Loops and containers, as a 2x2: when a general-purpose layout tool stops being adequate
==============================================================================

6,007 figures. 5.2 reports loops and containers separately. Crossed, they
are not additive: each one costs 'dot' something, and together they cost it more
than the sum.

 loop  container      n     ours      dot   oracle  gap(dot)
   no         no   1399    96.6%    83.5%    92.5%    +13.2
   no        yes   2048    88.9%    52.4%    68.4%    +36.5
  yes         no    827    92.3%    67.7%    82.1%    +24.5
  yes        yes   1733    74.3%    25.6%    33.8%    +48.7

Interaction on the gap (loop x container): +0.8 points: the two costs are
additive, not compounding. Each property independently costs 'dot' -23 points
(a container) and -11 points (a loop) of legibility relative to ours, and a graph with
both pays both.
'dot' falls from 83.5% on graphs with neither to 25.6% on graphs with both; ours
from 96.6% to 74.3%.

The same, by graph size, since the two grow together:

A rule from these three properties alone (no layout run): logistic fit of
'dot (tb, curved) fails the strict check' on boxes, loop and container.
  intercept      -5.121
  log(boxes+1)   +1.198   odds x3.31
